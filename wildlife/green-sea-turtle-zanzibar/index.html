Sea Turtles in Zanzibar: Where to See Them, Nesting Season

By Tim Hennig  ·  Updated 2026-07-01

The Short Answer

Green sea turtles are resident year-round at Mnemba Atoll, Zanzibar, feeding on seagrass beds most reliably spotted in the calm, clear water of December–March. Green turtles in Tanzania nest mainly from February to July, during the southeast monsoon, with the peak in April–May at the monitored sites; we found no verified peak for Mnemba alone. The Green Sea Turtle was reclassified from Endangered to Least Concern by the IUCN in 2025.

Where to actually see them: Mnemba Atoll

Zanzibar’s most reliable sea turtle encounters happen at Mnemba Atoll, a marine reserve off the island’s northeast coast where green sea turtles are resident year-round, feeding on seagrass beds close to the surface. Unlike a migratory species with a narrow seasonal window, these are turtles you can realistically encounter on most days of the year — the variable isn’t whether turtles are present, but water clarity and calm conditions.

December through March is consistently cited as the best window for snorkelling visibility — the dry season brings calmer seas and clearer water, with January–March specifically flagged as the peak for both conditions and turtle sightings. Outside this window, the encounters still happen, but sea conditions make snorkelling less pleasant and less productive.

When green turtles nest

The best-documented answer comes from Tanzania’s own monitoring rather than from tour-operator copy. Green turtles in Tanzania nest mainly from February to July, during the southeast monsoon. Sea Sense, the Tanzanian conservation NGO that runs community nest monitoring with the government, records the peak in April–May at Juani and Kigamboni. Few nests are laid in the later months of the year.

What we could not verify is a Mnemba-specific peak. Nest monitoring has run on Mnemba Island for years, but we found no published month-by-month figures for it, so we won’t invent one. Zanzibar tourism pages quote a range of other windows for individual beaches, and we haven’t been able to trace them to primary data.

If you’re planning a trip specifically to see nesting or hatchlings, aim for the February–July window and ask your lodge or conservation contact for their most recent local observations rather than relying on any single generic date range, including ours.

The ethics of watching a wild turtle

Sea turtles in Zanzibar are protected under both Tanzanian national law and international regulation — collecting, harming, or killing a turtle, or possessing or trading turtle products, is illegal. That legal baseline exists alongside a set of widely agreed ethical viewing practices for in-water encounters:

  • Maintain a respectful distance and never chase a turtle to get closer
  • Let the turtle approach on its own terms rather than swimming toward it
  • Never swim between a turtle and the surface — it needs to breathe
  • No touching, ever, regardless of how calm or apparently indifferent the animal seems
  • No flash photography
  • Never feed a wild turtle
  • Use reef-safe sun protection — ideally clothing rather than chemical sunscreen

That last point is more than a courtesy suggestion. Conservation groups working the Mnemba reef specifically flag sunscreen chemical runoff, combined with general human interference, as a contributor to reef damage in the turtles’ feeding grounds — a direct link between a visitor’s own sunscreen choice and the health of the habitat they came to see.

For nesting beaches specifically: no lights at night near a nesting or hatching event, no approaching a nest, and no directing torch or phone light at hatchlings, which navigate to the sea using light cues and can be fatally disoriented by artificial light. Several operators, including &Beyond’s Mnemba programme, run active hatchling-escort protocols — guests are sometimes invited to observe under strict guide supervision, which is the correct way to witness this rather than seeking it out independently.

A conservation status that’s actually good news

In 2025, the IUCN reclassified the Green Sea Turtle globally from Endangered to Least Concern, reflecting roughly 28% population growth since the 1970s — a genuine conservation success story attributable to decades of international protection efforts. The Southwest Indian Ocean subpopulation, which includes Zanzibar’s waters, carries the same Least Concern listing.

That is a real, verified improvement, and we’re not going to hedge it into something less positive than it is. A single global IUCN status doesn’t tell you what’s happening on one particular reef, so ask your operator what they are seeing locally.

The Mnarani Marine Turtles Conservation Pond in Nungwi is a related but distinct story — it’s a rescue and rehabilitation facility, not a wild encounter site. If you visit, understand it as a conservation and education facility rather than a benchmark for wild turtle abundance.

Hawksbill turtles: the less-discussed second species

Green sea turtles get most of the attention in Zanzibar, but hawksbill turtles are also present, though we have no verified nesting window for them. The same ethical viewing rules apply to both species; the practical difference for visitors is mainly which beach and season you’re likely to encounter hatchlings versus adults feeding on the reef.

Getting there

Mnemba Atoll sits off Zanzibar’s northeast coast, reachable by boat from lodges along the Matemwe and Nungwi coastline — most beach lodges in this stretch of Zanzibar run their own snorkelling excursions to the atoll, or can arrange access. There’s no need to travel elsewhere on the island specifically for turtles; this is one of the more accessible wildlife encounters in our entire East Africa coverage, requiring no charter flight or multi-day logistics chain, unlike Mafia Island’s whale shark season further south.

If you’re combining a turtle encounter with a broader East Africa itinerary — pairing Zanzibar’s marine wildlife with a Serengeti or Masai Mara safari — the Great Migration circuit runs on an entirely separate calendar, so the two don’t compete for the same travel window. Our migration calendar tracks both alongside each other month by month, and our Zanzibar travel guide covers the entry requirements, seasons, and logistics beneath the wildlife calendar itself. For the specific road-and-boat transfer to the atoll from Stone Town or the airport, see Mnemba Atoll snorkelling logistics.

What this means for your trip

If your goal is a reliable in-water turtle encounter, December through March at Mnemba Atoll is the safest bet on current evidence — resident turtles, calm water, good visibility. If your goal is specifically to witness nesting or hatchlings, be honest with yourself that the season data is genuinely unsettled, and your best move is asking your specific lodge for their current-year local observations rather than trusting any single published date range, including this one.

Frequently Asked Questions

When is the best time to see sea turtles in Zanzibar?

For in-water snorkelling encounters at Mnemba Atoll, December through March offers the calmest, clearest water and the highest sighting probability — turtles are resident year-round, but visibility conditions make this window most reliable. Nesting season is a separate question with genuinely conflicting source data (see below).

When do sea turtles nest in Zanzibar?

Green turtles in Tanzania nest mainly from February to July, during the southeast monsoon. Sea Sense monitoring at Juani and Kigamboni puts the peak in April–May, and few nests are laid late in the year. We found no published peak for Mnemba specifically, so we don't give one — ask your lodge for current local observations.

Is it safe and legal to swim with sea turtles in Zanzibar?

Yes, swimming near turtles is legal and commonly offered at Mnemba Atoll and other reef sites, but touching, chasing, or collecting turtles or turtle products is illegal under both Tanzanian national law and international regulation. Ethical viewing means maintaining distance, letting the turtle approach on its own terms, never blocking its path to the surface to breathe, and avoiding flash photography.

Are Zanzibar's sea turtles endangered?

The Green Sea Turtle's global status was downgraded from Endangered to Least Concern by the IUCN in 2025, reflecting roughly 28% population growth since the 1970s from decades of international conservation work. The Southwest Indian Ocean subpopulation, which includes Zanzibar, is separately listed as Least Concern.

What is the Mnarani Marine Turtles Conservation Pond?

It's a rescue and rehabilitation pond in Nungwi, Zanzibar. It functions differently from a wild-encounter site — it's a conservation and education facility, not a place to judge natural sighting probability.

Does reef sunscreen actually damage the coral turtles feed on?

Conservation groups working at Mnemba specifically advise against chemical sunscreens, citing reef damage from a combination of human interference and sunscreen chemical runoff, and recommend sun-protective clothing instead. This is a live, actionable choice every visitor makes, not a distant policy question.

What should I do if I find a nesting turtle or hatchlings?

Keep your distance, keep lights off (torches and phone flashlights disorient nesting turtles and hatchlings), and don't touch the nest or the animal. Several conservation programmes, including &Beyond Mnemba's, actively escort hatchlings to the sea to improve survival against predators — if you're offered the chance to observe this, follow your guide's instructions exactly.
