Dolphins in Zanzibar Menai Bay: Season, Numbers, Ethics

By Tim Hennig  ·  Updated 2026-07-03

The Short Answer

Menai Bay, Zanzibar, is home to resident Indo-Pacific bottlenose dolphins (Tursiops aduncus) and Indian Ocean humpback dolphins (Sousa plumbea, locally Critically Endangered and declining); spinner dolphins also occur. This is the one Beyond Paradise Adventures wildlife guide where we recommend caution rather than enthusiasm: official guidelines exist for approach distance and swimming, published research documents tourism pressure on the dolphins, and an acoustic study found dolphins avoid high-tourism sites by day.

Where to see them, and how reliable it actually is

Menai Bay Conservation Area, on Zanzibar’s southwest coast, hosts resident Indo-Pacific bottlenose dolphins (Tursiops aduncus) and Indian Ocean humpback dolphins (Sousa plumbea, locally Critically Endangered and declining); spinner dolphins (Stenella longirostris) also occur. This isn’t a seasonal migration story — the resident dolphins are there every month.

Sightings are common, though no operator can guarantee one. October through March is commonly cited as the best window, though this likely reflects calmer sea conditions and easier boat access rather than the dolphins being meaningfully more present during those months.

How many dolphins are actually there

Bottlenose-dolphin-specific mark-recapture surveys give us two data points a decade apart: an estimated 136 individuals in 2002, and 114 non-calf individuals in 2015. Both used similar mark-recapture methodology, and while we’re not going to overstate what a two-point comparison tells us, the direction is worth noting rather than ignoring.

A separate, higher figure of 200–400 individuals sometimes cited for the area refers to the combined resident group — both bottlenose and spinner dolphins counted together — which explains why it’s larger than the bottlenose-only surveys rather than contradicting them.

The part of this guide we’d rather not have to write

Every other wildlife guide on this site has been able to end on a note of “here’s how to do this responsibly, and it’s genuinely worth doing.” This one is more complicated, and we think you deserve the honest version rather than a marketing-friendly one.

The Menai Bay Conservation Area has official guidelines for approach distance and swimming. Ask your operator to show them.

In practice, published sources report that dolphin tourism in Menai Bay is largely unregulated and unmanaged. Operators have been documented chasing dolphins at high speed and permitting tourists to jump directly onto pods from the boat — behavior that is the opposite of any sensible approach guideline, not a minor infraction of it.

This isn’t speculation or a single disgruntled traveler’s complaint. A peer-reviewed acoustic study — Temple et al. (2016), published in PLOS ONE — found that dolphins avoid high-tourism sites by day. Separate research (Stensland & Berggren 2007) found that mothers with calves were the group most affected by boat approaches.

What responsible operator selection actually looks like here

Given the gap between the official guidelines and documented practice, operator selection matters more for this experience than almost any other on this site.

What to look for:

  • Explicit MBCA (Menai Bay Conservation Area) registration — ask directly, don’t assume
  • Morning-only tours (dolphins are more active, and there are fewer competing boats)
  • A stated one-boat-per-dolphin-group policy, treated as a hard rule rather than a best-effort guideline
  • Engines cut when dolphins approach the boat, not just when the boat approaches the dolphins
  • A briefing before you’re in the water that explicitly covers avoidance-behavior signs (direction change, speed increase, repeated submerging) and what happens if you see them — you get out, you don’t keep swimming

Red flags:

  • Multiple boats converging on a single dolphin group
  • Engines revving to keep pace with swimming dolphins
  • Guests permitted to swim or jump onto pods showing clear avoidance behavior
  • No pre-water briefing on the approach and swimming guidelines at all

Some conservation-linked tourism initiatives in the area have run boat-driver training programmes specifically covering safe approach procedures and dolphin behavior recognition — asking your operator whether their drivers have received this kind of training is a legitimate and useful question.

Spinner dolphins: the quieter second species

Spinner dolphins also occur in these waters and carry a global Least Concern IUCN status. They are documented as more wary of boat approaches than bottlenose dolphins, resting and traveling more during daytime hours. The same ethical standards apply, with extra caution warranted around any group that appears to be resting rather than actively socializing or feeding.

The conservation status, and what it means

The Indo-Pacific bottlenose dolphin was reclassified from Data Deficient to Near Threatened by the IUCN in a December 2019 assessment — a status shift that typically reflects improved data quality as much as a changing population trend, but Near Threatened is not a status to treat casually. Combined with the population survey trend and the documented tourism-disturbance research, this is a species and a specific site where “the animal is present and easy to find” and “the encounter is unambiguously fine” are two separate questions with two separate answers.

Getting there

Menai Bay is on Zanzibar’s southwest coast, most commonly accessed from Fumba or Kizimkazi village, both reachable by road from Stone Town in under an hour. Most dolphin tour operators are based in these two villages rather than requiring a separate booking from your main Zanzibar accommodation — ask your lodge to confirm which specific operator they book with, and hold them to the same MBCA-registration standard outlined above.

If your Zanzibar itinerary also includes Mnemba Atoll’s resident sea turtles, that’s a separate, better-regulated marine encounter on the island’s northeast coast — worth considering as an alternative or addition if the Menai Bay ethics situation gives you pause.

If you’re based in Paje or Jambiani for the trip, you can also take a Stone Town day trip — see our Paje and Jambiani transfer logistics if you’re combining the two.

Why we’re covering this so directly

Beyond Paradise Adventures exists to answer “is this ethical” honestly, not to sell the experience regardless of the answer — see our editorial policy for the full standard we hold ourselves to. Dolphin tourism in Menai Bay is a genuinely useful test case for that standard: the experience is popular, widely marketed, and easy to book, and the gap between the official guidelines and documented practice is exactly the kind of thing a decision-support guide should surface rather than smooth over.

None of this means the underlying experience is inherently harmful when done well. A single boat, morning timing, a briefed and disciplined driver, and immediate withdrawal at the first sign of avoidance behavior is a materially different encounter — for the dolphins and for you — than the high-speed, multi-boat pattern the research documents. The responsibility for making that distinction currently sits almost entirely with the operator you choose, which is precisely why we’re spending this much of the guide on operator selection rather than on describing what the water looks like.

What this means for your trip

This is not a guide telling you not to go. It’s a guide telling you that “go, but choose your operator with real scrutiny” is a materially different recommendation than the standard travel-blog version of this experience, and the difference matters to the animals involved. Ask the MBCA-registration question directly, book a morning slot, and treat any operator who can’t answer clearly as a pass.

Frequently Asked Questions

What's the best time to see dolphins in Menai Bay?

Dolphins are resident year-round, but October through March is cited as the peak window, likely coinciding with calmer seas and better boat access rather than the dolphins actually being more present. Morning tours are specifically recommended — dolphins are more active and fewer boats are on the water.

How likely am I to actually see dolphins?

Sightings are common, but no operator can guarantee one. That is about seeing dolphins, not about the quality or ethics of the encounter, which is a separate and more complicated question.

Is it ethical to swim with dolphins in Menai Bay?

This is genuinely complicated, and we're not going to oversimplify it. The Menai Bay Conservation Area has official guidelines for approach distance and swimming — ask your operator to show them. Published sources report that in practice, tourism in Menai Bay is largely unregulated, with many operators chasing dolphins at high speed and allowing tourists to jump directly onto pods. A peer-reviewed acoustic study (Temple et al. 2016, PLOS ONE) found that dolphins avoid high-tourism sites by day.

How many dolphins live in Menai Bay?

Bottlenose-dolphin-specific mark-recapture surveys estimated 136 individuals in 2002 and 114 non-calf individuals in 2015 — a decline worth noting, though the two studies used different methods and aren't a perfect apples-to-apples comparison. A separate estimate of 200–400 individuals refers to the combined resident group of bottlenose and spinner dolphins together, which is why it's a larger number than the bottlenose-only surveys.

How do I choose an ethical dolphin operator in Menai Bay?

Look for MBCA (Menai Bay Conservation Area) registration specifically, ask to see the official approach and swimming guidelines, insist on morning-only tours, and treat a single boat per dolphin group as the minimum acceptable standard — not the exception. Red flags include multiple boats converging on one pod, engines running to keep pace with the animals, and any operator letting guests enter the water once dolphins show avoidance behavior (direction changes, speed increases, or submerging repeatedly).

What is the dolphins' conservation status?

The Indo-Pacific bottlenose dolphin was reclassified from Data Deficient to Near Threatened by the IUCN in a December 2019 assessment. Indian Ocean humpback dolphins (Sousa plumbea) also live in Menai Bay and are locally Critically Endangered and declining. Spinner dolphins also occur here and carry a Least Concern listing globally.

Are spinner dolphins different from bottlenose dolphins in Menai Bay?

Yes — spinner dolphins rest and travel more during the day and are more sensitive to boat approaches. The same ethical operator standards apply to all dolphin species, but guides should give spinner dolphin groups, especially resting ones, extra distance.
